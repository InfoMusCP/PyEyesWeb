Synchronization#

Two dancers who move in unison, a quartet that breathes together, two hands that keep the same rhythm: synchronization is the degree to which the movements of several participants, or of several parts of the same body, are coordinated in time. There is no single measure of it, because coordination can mean different things: the signals rise and fall together, their phases stay locked, their discrete events occur at the same moments, their trajectories visit the same states. The seven features of this family are organised here by what is compared.

Notation is that of Notation, units and conventions: samples are indexed by \(n\), a window has \(N\) samples and slides by a hop \(H\). With one exception that is stated below, the measures work on such a window, return WARMING_UP until it is full and NO_OUTPUT between two hops (see Results, statuses and recorders), and count time in samples (lag, max_lag and event intervals), not in seconds. A pair of signals is passed to process one sample of each per call, and to run as two series of the same length. Inputs to the measures are one signal per participant: for 2D or 3D data, reduce each participant to a scalar first, for example the speed from Kinematics.

Synchronization measures assume that the coordination is stationary over the window. Choose the window so that it contains several cycles of the movement and so that the coordination does not change within it.

Phase#

For oscillatory movement, such as the swing of two arms or the steps of two walkers, synchronization is often a matter of phase: the signals may have different amplitudes and a constant lag and still be perfectly coordinated. The phase locking value (PLV) measures this [30]; it has been applied to the movement of groups in [4]. The PLV of Lachaux et al. averages over repeated trials at each instant; movement data has no repeated trials, so the feature computes the single-trial form, the PLV over time within a window, also called the mean phase coherence [31]. Each window of each signal is optionally band-pass filtered, its mean is removed, it is tapered with a Hann window, and the instantaneous phase \(\phi(n)\) is taken from the analytic signal obtained with the Hilbert transform,

\[ z_x(n)=x(n)+i\,\mathcal{H}[x(n)],\qquad \phi_x(n)=\arg z_x(n),\qquad \Delta\phi(n)=\phi_x(n)-\phi_y(n), \]

and the PLV is the length of the mean phasor of the phase difference over the middle 80 % of the \(N\) samples of the window (the 10 % at each end, distorted by the edges of the Hilbert transform, are left out, as in Mormann et al.),

\[ \mathrm{PLV}=\left|\frac{1}{N-2k}\sum_{n=k+1}^{N-k}e^{\,i\,\Delta\phi(n)}\right|\in[0,1],\qquad k=\lfloor 0.1N\rfloor . \]

It is 1 when the phase difference is constant over the window (perfect locking, whatever the offset), and close to 0 when it is spread uniformly (no phase relation). PhaseLockingValue implements it and returns phase_locking_value for the last complete window.

The Hilbert phase is only meaningful for narrow-band, oscillatory signals; Lachaux et al. band-pass at \(f\pm2\) Hz before taking it. On broadband data the value is not a phase relation: independent white noise gives about 0.08 on 200 samples, but independent random walks about 0.57, because each window is dominated by one slow trend. Nothing is filtered unless low_frequency and high_frequency (with the sampling_rate) are given, so either band-limit the signals first or pass the band, and use windows of several whole periods. A constant signal has no phase: the result is INVALID_INPUT.

>>> from pyeyesweb.analysis_primitives import PhaseLockingValue
>>> time = np.arange(200) / 100.0
>>> reference = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3.0 * time)
>>> locked = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3.0 * time + 0.5)                 # same rhythm, shifted by 0.5 rad
>>> drifting = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 4.3 * time)                     # a different rhythm
>>> plv = PhaseLockingValue(window=200, low_frequency=1.0, high_frequency=10.0, sampling_rate=100.0)
>>> round(float(plv.run(reference, locked).phase_locking_value[-1]), 2)
0.99
>>> plv.reset()
>>> round(float(plv.run(reference, drifting).phase_locking_value[-1]), 2)
0.04

Event series#

Many movement data are discrete events rather than signals: the onsets of gestures, the beats of a performer, the peaks found by ScalarPeakDetector. Event synchronization measures the coordination of two such series without assuming a fixed rhythm [32]. In the input a non-zero sample is an event. With \(m_x\) and \(m_y\) events in the first and in the second series, and \(c(y|x)\) the number of events of the second series that follow an event of the first within max_lag samples (and \(c(x|y)\) the converse), the feature EventSynchronization reports

\[ Q=\frac{c(x|y)+c(y|x)}{\sqrt{m_x m_y}}\in[0,1],\qquad q=\frac{c(y|x)-c(x|y)}{\sqrt{m_x m_y}}\in[-1,1]. \]

\(Q=1\) means that every event of each series has a partner in the other. \(q>0\) means that the first series leads, \(q<0\) that the second does; simultaneous events count for \(Q\) but not for \(q\). The outputs are synchronization (\(Q\)) and delay (\(q\)).

The feature works on consecutive windows that do not overlap: hop equals window (its default) and any other value raises ValueError. It gives one result per window (OK once per window) and no NO_OUTPUT, the exception to the sliding windows of the family. By default each window is evaluated on its own; with reset_every_buffer=False the counts accumulate across windows, so the value of a window describes the whole recording up to its end (the cumulative index of the paper).

\(Q\) depends strongly on max_lag: the same two series give a low \(Q\) with a small max_lag and a \(Q\) close to 1 with a large one, so choose max_lag from the lags that count as simultaneous in your data.

Corrected in the core: the result follows the definition above, with the coincidence window \(0<\text{lag}\le\) max_lag, the sign of the delay given here, and each event credited exactly once.

EventSynchronizationMECS (multi-event-class synchronization) extends the measure to number_of_inputs series that carry events of several classes (for example one channel per type of gesture) [33]. For every class and every pair of series, the coincidences \(S_{ij}\) are the pairs of events less than max_lag samples apart, weighted by the kernel (by default linear, \(1-d/\)max_lag, the kernel of the paper); with \(m_i\) and \(m_j\) the event counts of the two series the pair term is \(\min\!\big(1,S_{ij}/\sqrt{m_im_j}\big)\), and the synchronization of the class is the mean of the pair terms over the \(M(M-1)/2\) pairs of the \(M\) series. The normalisation by \(\sqrt{m_im_j}\) departs from the paper’s \(m_im_j\), with which two identical trains never reach 1. It returns the synchronization of each class and the number of events of each class in the window, summed over the series, and also needs non-overlapping windows. As in the paper, the last max_lag samples of a window are carried into the next one, so pairs across the boundary are found; it has no reset_every_buffer, and it depends on max_lag in the same way. Only the synchronization within each class is computed (the former “version 2” of the algorithm); the inter-class part of the paper is not implemented.

>>> from pyeyesweb.analysis_primitives import EventSynchronization
>>> first, second = np.zeros(60), np.zeros(60)
>>> first[[5, 25, 45]] = 1                    # three events of the first performer
>>> second[[7, 27, 56]] = 1                   # the second follows two of them within 3 samples
>>> record = EventSynchronization(window=60, max_lag=3).run(first, second)
>>> round(float(record.synchronization[-1]), 2), round(float(record.delay[-1]), 2)
(0.67, 0.67)

Recurrence#

Recurrence analysis compares a movement with itself or with another movement in the space of states [34], and the lab uses it to measure the synchronization of a group [10]. For a window of states \(\mathbf{x}_n\) (one value per channel, for example the posture of a participant), the recurrence plot is the binary matrix

\[ R_{ij}=\Theta\big(\epsilon_i-\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_p\big),\qquad \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_p=\Big(\sum_c|v_c|^p\Big)^{1/p}, \]

with \(\Theta\) the Heaviside step, \(\Theta(0)=1\) (a distance equal to \(\epsilon\) is a recurrence, as in [34], eq. 10), \(p\) the order and \(\epsilon_i\) the threshold of row \(i\) (epsilon, or one per row with use_multiple_epsilons, in the recurrence plot only). The diagonal is 1. A periodic movement draws diagonal lines, whose spacing is the period; a movement that stays in one posture draws blocks. RecurrencePlot returns the matrix of the last complete window as recurrence_matrix, of shape \((N,N)\).

The cross-recurrence plot compares two multichannel signals with the same channels, for example two participants performing the same gesture: \(R_{ij}=\Theta(\epsilon-\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{y}_j\rVert_p)\), with \(\mathbf{x}\) from the first signal and \(\mathbf{y}\) from the second, and one threshold \(\epsilon\) (epsilon) for every row. Long diagonal lines mean that the two follow the same trajectory, possibly with a time shift, and the offset of the line from the main diagonal is that shift. CrossRecurrencePlot returns cross_recurrence_matrix. In both, the core corrected the order, which used to be ignored (the distance was always Euclidean).

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from pyeyesweb.analysis_primitives import CrossRecurrencePlot, RecurrencePlot

n = np.arange(100)
phase = 2 * np.pi * n / 25
leader = np.column_stack([np.sin(phase), np.cos(phase)])                          # a circular movement, 2 channels
follower = np.column_stack([np.sin(phase - 0.8), np.cos(phase - 0.8)])            # the same, 0.8 rad (about 3 samples) later

self_plot = RecurrencePlot(window=100, epsilon=0.2).run(leader).recurrence_matrix[-1]
cross_plot = CrossRecurrencePlot(window=100, epsilon=0.2).run(leader, follower).cross_recurrence_matrix[-1]

fig, (left, right) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(6.4, 3.2))
left.imshow(self_plot, origin="lower", cmap="Greys")
left.set_title("recurrence plot")
right.imshow(cross_plot, origin="lower", cmap="Greys")
right.set_title("cross-recurrence plot")
for axis in (left, right):
    axis.set_xlabel("sample j")
left.set_ylabel("sample i")
fig.tight_layout()

The first plot shows diagonal lines spaced by the period of 25 samples; in the second the diagonals are displaced from the main one by the delay between the two movements (0.8 rad of a cycle of 25 samples, about 3 samples).

The recurrence plot is a picture. GeneralisedAutoCorrelation reduces the recurrence of a window of \(N\) multichannel states to a number as a function of the lag \(\tau\) in samples: the recurrence rate

\[ \mathrm{RR}(\tau)=\frac1{N-\tau}\,\#\big\{\,i<N-\tau:\ \lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_{i+\tau}\rVert\le\epsilon\,\big\}, \]

the fraction of states that return within \(\epsilon\) after \(\tau\) samples. A periodic movement has peaks of \(\mathrm{RR}\) at the multiples of its period. With output_type="function_of_tau" the feature returns the rates at the lags 1 to max_lag (and the lags in taus); with output_type="scalar" it returns the rate at the single lag lag.

>>> from pyeyesweb.analysis_primitives import GeneralisedAutoCorrelation
>>> states = np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(200) / 50)[:, None]      # a movement with a period of 50 samples
>>> for lag in (25, 50):
...     rate = GeneralisedAutoCorrelation(window=200, output_type="scalar", lag=lag, epsilon=0.1).run(states).recurrence_rate[-1]
...     print(lag, round(float(rate[0]), 2))
25 0.04
50 1.0

Group synchronization#

For a group of \(M\) participants the question is how the members are coordinated pair by pair.

Correlation. The starting point is the matrix \(C\) of the Pearson correlations \(C_{kl}=\rho(s_k,s_l)\) of the signals (one per participant or per body segment) over a window. It is numpy.corrcoef: the entries are signed and lie in \([-1,1]\), with 1 on the diagonal. A signal that is constant over the window has no defined correlation, and its row and column are NaN.

Direct synchronization. Two participants can correlate only because both follow a third one. With \(P=C^{-1}\), PartialRecurrenceSynchronization removes the part of each correlation that the other signals explain and returns the absolute partial correlation

\[ S_{kl}=\frac{|P_{kl}|}{\sqrt{P_{kk}P_{ll}}}, \]

[10] (eq. 4). It takes the matrix as input, so it is applied to the output of numpy.corrcoef; it has no window of its own. In the paper the matrix holds the CPR index of each pair, numpy.corrcoef of the recurrence-rate curves of GeneralisedAutoCorrelation (Varni eq. 3). A matrix with a NaN entry, a matrix too ill-conditioned to invert (condition number above \(10^{12}\), as for constant or perfectly collinear signals) and a matrix that is not positive definite (a diagonal entry of \(P\) not positive, so not a correlation matrix) are INVALID_INPUT.

In the example the signal \(b\) follows \(a\) and \(c\) follows \(b\) with noise. All three are correlated, but \(a\) and \(c\) only through \(b\), which the partial correlation shows.

>>> from pyeyesweb.analysis_primitives import PartialRecurrenceSynchronization
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
>>> a = rng.normal(size=300)
>>> b = a + 0.7 * rng.normal(size=300)
>>> c = b + 0.7 * rng.normal(size=300)
>>> correlations = np.corrcoef(np.column_stack([a, b, c])[-300:].T)      # the last 300 samples, one row per signal
>>> correlations.round(2)
array([[1.  , 0.81, 0.68],
       [0.81, 1.  , 0.85],
       [0.68, 0.85, 1.  ]])
>>> PartialRecurrenceSynchronization().process(correlations).synchronization_matrix.round(2)
array([[1.  , 0.61, 0.04],
       [0.61, 1.  , 0.7 ],
       [0.04, 0.7 , 1.  ]])

Choosing a measure#

  • Continuous signals, oscillatory and narrow-band: the phase locking value. Continuous signals with an arbitrary coupling: the nonlinear interdependence, or the partial correlation for a group.

  • Discrete events with a known tolerance in samples: event synchronization (two series) or MECS (several classes). Events without a natural tolerance: SPIKE-synchronization.

  • Whole-body or multichannel states, to see where and when two movements revisit the same posture: cross-recurrence.

Practical notes#

  • Reduce every participant to a scalar signal (or one vector per sample for the recurrence measures) and give all participants the same sampling rate, preferably from one clock. Resample before the measure if the streams differ.

  • The window should hold several cycles of the movement. window, lag and max_lag are in samples: \(N=\lceil T f_s\rceil\) for a window of \(T\) seconds.

  • A NaN or infinite value gives INVALID_INPUT and no output; the Notes of each feature in the API reference give the exact rule.

  • All values are dimensionless. The synchronization of surrogate signals (shuffled, or shifted by more than a cycle) gives a baseline for a significance threshold that no measure provides on its own.
