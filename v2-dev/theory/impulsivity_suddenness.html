Impulsivity and suddenness#

An impulsive movement is one that starts abruptly and without preparation, like a flick of the hand or a strike; a sudden movement is one whose speed changes in bursts, a quick start and stop in the middle of calm. Both are mid-level qualities of movement in the conceptual model (Conceptual framework): they describe a movement unit of about a second, not one frame, and they have been studied as qualities that observers perceive and that interactive systems can recognise [3, 20, 21].

The family has three features. Suddenness classifies the distribution of the speed of a trajectory over a window. SuddennessImpulsivity combines the suddenness with the sharpness of a turn of the path to give an impulsivity index of the trajectory. ContractionImpulsivity takes another route: it detects short energetic gestures from the quantity of motion of the whole body and measures how fast the body contracts or expands during them. The symbols are those of Notation, units and conventions.

Suddenness#

Definition#

The speed of a point between two consecutive samples is \(v_t = \lVert \mathbf{p}_{t+1} - \mathbf{p}_t \rVert\). A movement made of sudden starts and stops does not have a Gaussian distribution of speed: most of the time the point is slow or at rest, and now and then it is very fast, so the distribution has a heavy tail. The \(\alpha\)-stable distributions are the family that has the Gaussian as one member and describes heavy-tailed and skewed distributions in general. They are parametrised by

  • \(\alpha \in (0, 2]\), the stability index, which sets the thickness of the tails: \(\alpha = 2\) is the Gaussian and a smaller \(\alpha\) gives heavier tails;

  • \(\beta\), the skewness, positive for a heavier tail on the side of large values;

  • \(\gamma\), the scale, a positive number of the order of the spread of the distribution.

Suddenness fits these parameters to the speeds of a window by McCulloch’s quantile method. With \(x_5, x_{25}, x_{50}, x_{75}, x_{95}\) the quantiles of the speeds of the window,

\[ \nu_\alpha = \frac{x_{95} - x_5}{x_{75} - x_{25}}, \qquad \nu_\beta = \frac{x_{95} + x_5 - 2 x_{50}}{x_{95} - x_5}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{x_{75} - x_{25}}{x_{75} - x_{50}}, \]

and \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are obtained from \(\nu_\alpha\) and \(\nu_\beta\) by interpolation in McCulloch’s tables. The window is classified as sudden when

\[ \gamma \left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{2}\right) \beta \ge 0 , \]

which, since \(\gamma\) is positive and \(\alpha \le 2\), means a non-negative skewness \(\beta\), or a Gaussian (\(\alpha = 2\)). In words: the movement is sudden when the speeds are mostly low with occasional bursts, so that the tail is on the side of the large values, and it is not sudden when the speeds are mostly high with occasional stops, a negative skew. A Gaussian distribution has no tail to speak of: a window whose speeds are all equal, a point at rest or at constant speed, gives the Gaussian fallback (\(\alpha = 2\), \(\beta = 0\), \(\gamma = 0\)) and is classified as sudden.

The speeds are differences between consecutive samples, so timestamps do not enter the computation, and \(\gamma\) is a ratio of quantile differences, so it is dimensionless.

The feature#

Suddenness takes one position per call, the point of a trajectory in two or three dimensions (a frame with several markers is rejected: average the markers beforehand if one trajectory is wanted). It keeps window positions (at least 6, because five speeds are needed) and computes every hop. Its outputs are is_sudden (boolean), alpha, beta and gamma. The parameter method selects the fit: "constrained" (the default) restricts \(\nu_\alpha\) and \(\nu_\beta\) to the range of McCulloch’s tables and always gives a result; "quantile" uses the unrestricted values and linear percentiles, and gives INVALID_INPUT, with no outputs, when the window is outside the range of the tables. The status is WARMING_UP until the first window is full and NO_OUTPUT between windows.

Two trajectories of 20 steps along a line illustrate the classification. The first is slow, with two bursts; the second is fast and steady, with three brief stops:

>>> from pyeyesweb.mid_level import Suddenness, SuddennessImpulsivity
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
>>> n = 20
>>> def corner(speeds, turn=True):
...     """A path that moves along x for half of the steps and then along y (or keeps going along x)."""
...     steps = np.zeros((n, 2))
...     steps[: n // 2, 0] = speeds[: n // 2]
...     steps[n // 2:, 1 if turn else 0] = speeds[n // 2:]
...     return np.vstack([[0.0, 0.0], np.cumsum(steps, axis=0)])
>>> slow = 0.01 + 0.003 * rng.random(n)
>>> bursts = slow.copy()
>>> bursts[[3, 4, 13]] = [0.3, 0.25, 0.3]                              # three fast steps in a slow movement
>>> steady = 0.1 + 0.003 * rng.random(n)
>>> steady[[3, 9, 15]] = 0.003                                          # three brief stops in a fast one
>>> for name, speeds in [("bursts", bursts), ("steady with stops", steady)]:
...     last = Suddenness(window=21, dims=2).run(corner(speeds))[-1]
...     print(f"{name:18s} sudden={bool(last.is_sudden)}  alpha={last.alpha:.2f}  beta={last.beta:.2f}")
bursts             sudden=True  alpha=0.52  beta=1.24
steady with stops  sudden=False  alpha=0.52  beta=-1.19

Both have the same low \(\alpha\), that is heavy tails, and the sign of the skewness separates them.

Impulsivity from the trajectory#

An impulsive movement is not only sudden in time: it also changes direction sharply, like the stroke of a hammer that stops and returns. SuddennessImpulsivity captures this as the product of a spatial and a temporal term over a window of one trajectory [3, 20],

\[ I = D \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{sudden}], \]

where \(\mathbf{1}[\text{sudden}]\) is the classification of Suddenness on the speeds of the window and \(D \in [0, 1]\) is the direction change, computed from the first, the middle (index \(N // 2\)) and the last position of the window, \(\mathbf{p}_0\), \(\mathbf{p}_m\) and \(\mathbf{p}_{N-1}\). With \(\theta\) the angle between \(\mathbf{p}_m - \mathbf{p}_0\) and \(\mathbf{p}_{N-1} - \mathbf{p}_m\) (0 for a straight path, \(\pi\) for a reversal) and \(a = 1 - \theta / \pi\),

\[\begin{split} D = \begin{cases} 1 - \lvert a - 0.5 \rvert / \varepsilon & \lvert a - 0.5 \rvert < \varepsilon, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \end{split}\]

so that \(D\) is 1 for a right-angle turn and falls to 0 for a straight path or a reversal, with the default \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) (direction_change_epsilon); a smaller \(\varepsilon\) keeps only the turns close to a right angle. This is the cosine measure of DirectionChange, described in Kinematics and energy. Either factor being zero makes the index zero: a sharp corner traced slowly and evenly is not sudden, and a sudden movement along a straight line does not turn. The output impulsivity_index is dimensionless, in \([0, 1]\). Both terms are evaluated on the same single trajectory. Corrected in the core: the original block took the direction change of the mean of all the signals and the suddenness of the first signal only.

The inputs, parameters and statuses are those of Suddenness (window of at least 6, hop, dims, method). With the paths of the example above:

>>> def impulsivity(path):
...     return round(float(SuddennessImpulsivity(window=21, dims=2).run(path)[-1].impulsivity_index), 2)
>>> impulsivity(corner(bursts)), impulsivity(corner(bursts, turn=False)), impulsivity(corner(steady))
(1.0, 0.0, 0.0)

The bursts that turn at a right angle give the largest value. The same bursts along a straight line give 0, because there is no turn, and the steady movement with stops gives 0 because it is not sudden.

Impulsivity from the body: the contraction index#

The second approach uses the whole body instead of one trajectory. It needs two signals that other software supplies and that are normalised to \([0, 1]\): the quantity of motion, how much the body moves in a frame, and the contraction index, how contracted or expanded the posture is (high when the limbs are close to the body). A gesture is a run of frames with the quantity of motion above motion_threshold (0.01 by default), closed by the first frame at or below it. It is impulsive when it lasts at most gesture_duration frames (25 by default) and its peak quantity of motion reaches peak_threshold (0.02). On the frame that closes an impulsive gesture, ContractionImpulsivity gives the range of the contraction index over the gesture divided by its duration,

\[ I = \frac{\max_t CI_t - \min_t CI_t}{T / f_s}, \]

where \(CI_t\) is the contraction index, \(T\) the number of frames from the start of the gesture to the closing frame, inclusive, and \(f_s\) the sampling_rate. It is the rate, per second, at which the body contracts or expands during the gesture, and it is not negative; without a sampling rate it is per frame. On every other frame both outputs, impulsivity_index and gesture_duration (the length of the gesture, in frames), are 0. A pair of inputs outside \([0, 1]\) is ignored and gives zeros. The status is OK for every sample and INVALID_INPUT for a timestamp that does not increase.

In the example a short energetic gesture of five frames contracts the body from 0.5 to 0.8 and back. It is closed on the sixth frame, and the index is the range 0.3 over \(5 / 25 = 0.2\) s, that is 1.5 per second:

>>> from pyeyesweb.mid_level import ContractionImpulsivity
>>> quantity_of_motion = [0, 0.05, 0.2, 0.3, 0.1, 0, 0]
>>> contraction_index = [0.5, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 0.7, 0.5, 0.5]
>>> record = ContractionImpulsivity(sampling_rate=25.0).run(quantity_of_motion, contraction_index)
>>> record.impulsivity_index.round(2), record.gesture_duration
(array([0. , 0. , 0. , 0. , 0. , 1.5, 0. ]), array([0., 0., 0., 0., 0., 5., 0.]))

A gesture that goes on for more than gesture_duration frames, however energetic, is not impulsive, and the index stays at 0.

ContractionImpulsivity has a value only on the frame that closes an impulsive gesture, and the stream is zero between gestures. For a time series of the impulsivity of a performance, take the maximum over a stretch or the number of non-zero values.

Practical notes#

  • Window length. The window of the two trajectory features holds one movement: the quantiles of the speeds, and the first, the middle and the last position, only make sense on a unit. With 21 positions at 50 Hz the window is 0.4 s. A window that holds several turns takes only the turn between the first, middle and last position.

  • Hop. With a hop of 1 the classification is renewed at every sample; a hop of the length of the window gives one value per window, with NO_OUTPUT between them.

  • Noise. The speed is a difference of positions, and noise in the position changes its distribution. A trajectory with jitter should be smoothed beforehand (Kinematics and energy).

  • Rest. A window at rest or at constant speed is classified as sudden by the Gaussian fallback, but its direction change is 0, so its impulsivity is 0 (status OK). Use the energy or the motion duration (Gesture segmentation) to ignore stillness.

  • Several markers. Both trajectory features take one point. Average the markers of a body, or choose one, before the call.
